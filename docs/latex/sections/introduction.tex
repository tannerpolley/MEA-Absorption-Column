    \section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}
        Aqueous monoethanolamine (MEA) absorption remains a common reference process for post-combustion CO$_2$ capture because it combines mature solvent chemistry with well-studied packed-column equipment. The same maturity also raises the standard for new modeling papers. A useful contribution must do more than reproduce a single pilot-plant case with established correlations; it must clarify what is being benchmarked, which assumptions are changed, which data support the conclusions, and where the model remains incomplete.

        The present work addresses that narrower need. It uses a transparent steady-state MEA absorber model to benchmark numerical solution paths and CO$_2$ fugacity driving-force assumptions under a common set of governing equations. The physical submodels are intentionally described as a simplified rate-based model assembled from literature correlations. The paper does not claim that those correlations, the PC-SAFT family of equations of state, or the boundary-value algorithms are new. The specific thermodynamic contribution is, to the authors' knowledge, the first use of PC-SAFT fugacity-coefficient calculations on both the vapor and liquid sides of the CO$_2$ driving force within a solved packed-column absorber benchmark. The broader contribution is the reproducible comparison produced when the equations, NCCC case inputs, initialization rules, solver settings, thermodynamic model, acceptance criteria, and reporting structure are held fixed.

        This framing responds to two practical needs in absorber modeling. First, solver behavior should be reported with enough numerical detail that capture and temperature predictions can be reproduced rather than treated as isolated case studies. Second, replacing a Henry-law CO$_2$ driving force with an equation-of-state fugacity calculation can change both prediction and runtime; that effect should be isolated before changing reaction chemistry, transport correlations, or calibration factors. The benchmark therefore evaluates shooting, finite-difference, and collocation BVP solution paths, and it compares a Henry-law baseline with a PC-SAFT fugacity-coefficient calculation that changes only the CO$_2$ fugacity driving force. This comparison is designed around the practical tradeoff between a very fast but simplified Henry-law model, more parameter-intensive activity-coefficient approaches, and an EOS-based PC-SAFT calculation that offers a physically structured middle ground.

        \subsection{Literature Review}

        Rate-based MEA absorber modeling is a mature area. Foundational and subsequent studies have coupled chemical reaction, vapor-liquid equilibrium, transport, hydraulics, and energy balances to predict pilot-scale CO$_2$ absorption behavior \cite{Freguia2003,Mores2012,Zhang2009}. Related process-simulation studies have extended this framework toward load variation, solvent comparison, process control, and design analysis \cite{Moioli2012,Harun2012a,Greer2008,Rodriguez2014}. These studies establish the relevance of mechanistic absorber models, but they also show that a new model must be evaluated by its reproducibility, validation evidence, and numerical transparency rather than by novelty alone.

        Validation practice is central to this manuscript. Prior MEA absorber studies have used pilot-scale or campaign data to constrain and evaluate rate-based models, including packed-column validation, hydraulic and mass-transfer modeling, and uncertainty-oriented procedures \cite{Razi2013,Chinen2018,Morgan2018,Morgan2020}. A single successful case can show that a model executes, but it does not demonstrate reliability across the operating range. The benchmark therefore reports case-wise capture error, temperature-profile error where measurements are available, boundary residuals, solver messages, and runtime across the available one-bed NCCC validation cases.

        Thermodynamic model choice is another source of ambiguity. Electrolyte activity-coefficient models such as eNRTL can represent MEA chemistry with high fidelity but require many fitted interaction parameters and careful calibration \cite{Zhang2011}. PC-SAFT offers an equation-of-state route for associating systems \cite{Gross2001,Cameretti2005}. PC-SAFT and SAFT-family models have also been applied to CO$_2$ solubility in aqueous MEA with explicit association-scheme choices \cite{Baygi2015,Najafloo2018}, while recent acid-gas absorption studies demonstrate the continuing use of EOS frameworks for reactive aqueous amine systems \cite{Cleeton2020,Bulow2021a}. That literature motivates an EOS-based driving-force comparison, but it does not by itself validate the present column model. In this work, PC-SAFT is not introduced as a new thermodynamic theory or as a complete MEA thermodynamic replacement. It is used as a CO$_2$ fugacity-coefficient calculation so that the effect of replacing the Henry-law driving force can be evaluated without simultaneously changing reaction chemistry, enhancement factors, or transport correlations. This makes the PC-SAFT calculation valuable as a pragmatic middle-ground model: more physically grounded than an ideal or Henry-law driving-force closure, but still light enough for repeated column simulations when only the fugacity driving force is being tested.

        Numerical method comparisons in absorber models must also be interpreted carefully. Shooting, finite-difference, and collocation BVP methods are standard tools, but their behavior depends on scaling, initialization, mesh size, residual definitions, and physical-domain constraints. The manuscript therefore treats the numerical methods as benchmarked solver configurations rather than as new algorithms. The value of the comparison is that the same model, data, and acceptance criteria expose where each method is fast, where it is fragile, and where solver convergence should not be confused with physical validation.

    \subsection{Scope and Contributions}
        This literature context shows a mature modeling landscape rather than an absence of MEA absorber models. The remaining gap is reproducibility and controlled comparison: many studies use different thermodynamic assumptions, different solver formulations, different acceptance criteria, and different validation subsets, making solver behavior difficult to distinguish from model-form error. This work therefore makes four bounded contributions.
        \begin{itemize}
            \item It provides a transparent benchmark in which shooting, finite-difference, and collocation BVP solution paths are evaluated against the same absorber equations and case data.
            \item It expands validation beyond the original Case 3C illustration to all seven one-bed NCCC C cases, with capture-error, temperature-profile, runtime, and residual evidence.
            \item It introduces, to the authors' knowledge, the first PC-SAFT CO$_2$ fugacity-coefficient calculation embedded in a solved packed-column absorber benchmark, computing both vapor- and liquid-side CO$_2$ fugacities while keeping chemical equilibrium, enhancement factors, transport, hydraulics, and balances fixed.
            \item It records solver settings, convergence messages, residual norms, runtime, and benchmark gates so the numerical comparison remains auditable and reproducible.
        \end{itemize}
        The claim is therefore deliberately conservative. The model is useful for reproducible benchmarking and hypothesis testing, but it is not presented as a calibrated industrial process simulator or as a full electrolyte-reactive MEA absorber.
