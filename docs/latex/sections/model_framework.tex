    \section{Model Framework}
    \label{sec:model-framework}
        Solvent-based post-combustion carbon capture (PCC) models describe how $\COtwo$ moves from the vapor phase into the liquid solvent inside a packed column. That transfer is driven by thermodynamics and limited by transport, reaction kinetics, hydraulics, and heat release. Local temperature, pressure, composition, and flow rates determine the properties used in those submodels. The governing mass and energy balances then track how the liquid and vapor streams change with packed height.

        \begin{figure}
            \centering
            \includegraphics[width=12cm]{figures/model-framework-flowchart.png}
            \caption{Absorber model structure. Flow, composition, temperature, and pressure determine thermodynamic and transport properties; those properties set interfacial fluxes; and the fluxes close the material and energy balances solved by the numerical methods.}\label{fig:Model_Framework_Flowchart}
        \end{figure}
        Figure~\ref{fig:Model_Framework_Flowchart}, adapted from Luo \cite{Luo2017}, outlines the model structure used for the MEA absorber benchmark. The calculation begins with flow, composition, pressure, and temperature variables for the vapor and liquid phases. These variables are used to evaluate thermodynamic, transport, and hydraulic properties, which define interfacial fluxes and close the governing balances. This structure is important for the present study because it allows the numerical method and CO$_2$ fugacity model to be changed while the rest of the absorber equations remain fixed.
        
        Thermodynamic modeling includes both chemical equilibrium and vapor--liquid equilibrium (VLE). The chemical-equilibrium calculation determines true species concentrations from apparent component amounts and the retained reaction set. The VLE calculation then supplies the fugacities used in the interfacial driving force. The model switches the CO$_2$ driving-force calculation between the baseline Henry-law expression and an ePC-SAFT fugacity-coefficient calculation. In this work, Henry-law closure is the fast baseline, while ePC-SAFT provides an electrolyte equation-of-state CO$_2$ fugacity closure. The liquid-side calculation uses ePC-SAFT for the MEA--CO$_2$--water electrolyte state, whereas the vapor-side calculation uses the same fugacity framework with only neutral species present. Chemical equilibrium, enhancement factor, transport correlations, hydraulics, and balances remain unchanged so that the thermodynamic comparison isolates the fugacity calculation rather than introducing a second absorber model. The ePC-SAFT rows require additional density and fugacity-coefficient evaluations, but the reported runtimes remain in the repeated-simulation regime. Once the thermodynamic terms are evaluated, they are combined with hydraulic variables, transfer coefficients, heat transfer, and the enhancement factor to calculate species and enthalpy fluxes. These fluxes close the governing mass and energy balances.

        \subsection{Thermodynamics}\label{subsec:thermodynamics}
            The thermodynamics section establishes the chemical and phase behavior that sets the local $\COtwo$ driving force in the MEA solvent system. It begins by solving chemical equilibrium for true species concentrations. Those concentrations then feed transport-property calculations and the vapor--liquid fugacity calculation. Together, the speciation and VLE models determine the local mass-transfer driving force used by the packed-column balances.
        
            \subsubsection{Chemical Equilibrium}
                The liquid-phase chemical-equilibrium calculation converts apparent component concentrations into true species concentrations. These true species are used in the transport correlations, enhancement factor, and fugacity calculations. The calculation begins with the apparent species concentrations and equilibrium constants for the two retained reactions:
                \begin{align}
                    & 2 \MEA+\COtwo \leftrightarrow \MEAH +\MEACOO
                    && \text{stoichiometric reaction} \\
                    & \MEA+\COtwo+\HtwoO \leftrightarrow \MEAH +\HCOthree
                    && \text{stoichiometric reaction}
                \end{align}
                This reaction set represents a simplification of the solution chemistry that was developed by Hilliard \cite{Hilliard2008} for typical $\COtwo$ capture process conditions where concentrations of other ions (\ce{H3O+}, \ce{OH-}, \ce{CO3^{2-}}) are negligibly low.

                This reaction set gives the following stoichiometric matrix $\mathbf{\nu}_{i,r}$:
                \begin{align}
                    \mathbf{\nu}_{i, r} = 
                    \begin{bmatrix}
                    -1 & -1 \\
                    -2 & -1 \\
                     0 & -1 \\
                     1 &  1 \\
                     1 &  0 \\
                     0 &  1
                    \end{bmatrix}
                    && \text{dimensionless}
                \end{align}
                The equilibrium constants are found through fitting speciation data to the following equation.
                
                \begin{align}
                    \log K_r = a_r + \frac{b_r}{T_l} + c_r \ln(T_l) + d_r T_l
                    && \text{dimensionless}
                \end{align}

                 where $r$ represents a reaction number.
                 
                 The reaction quotient in terms of concentration is the following. 
                 
                 \begin{align}
                     Q_r &= \prod_i \text{C}_i^{\nu_{i,r}}
                     && \left((\unit{mol\,m^{-3}})^{\sum_i\nu_{i,r}}\right)
                  \end{align}
                  Therefore, the relationship
                 \begin{align}
                     Q_r &= K_r
                     && \left((\unit{mol\,m^{-3}})^{\sum_i\nu_{i,r}}\right)
                 \end{align}
                is used for both reactions alongside the material and charge-balance equations derived from Akula \cite{Akula2023}. The tabulated constants are converted onto the absorber's \(\unit{mol\,m^{-3}}\) concentration basis before solving the residual equations.

                \begin{align}
                    C^{\liq}_{\ce{CO2},0} &= C^{\liq}_{\ce{CO2}} + C^{\liq}_{\ce{MEAH}^{+}}
                    && \left(\unit{mol\,m^{-3}}\right)\\
                    C^{\liq}_{\ce{MEA},0} &= C^{\liq}_{\ce{MEA}} + C^{\liq}_{\ce{MEAH}^{+}} + C^{\liq}_{\ce{MEACOO}^{-}}
                    && \left(\unit{mol\,m^{-3}}\right)\\
                    C^{\liq}_{\ce{H2O},0} &= C^{\liq}_{\ce{H2O}} + C^{\liq}_{\ce{MEAH}^{+}} - C^{\liq}_{\ce{MEACOO}^{-}}
                    && \left(\unit{mol\,m^{-3}}\right)\\
                    C^{\liq}_{\ce{MEAH}^{+}} &= C^{\liq}_{\ce{MEACOO}^{-}} + C^{\liq}_{\ce{HCO3}^{-}}
                    && \left(\unit{mol\,m^{-3}}\right)\\
                    \textbf{C} &= \left[
                         C^{\liq}_{\COtwo}, C^{\liq}_{\MEA}, C^{\liq}_{\HtwoO}, C^{\liq}_{\MEAH}, C^{\liq}_{\MEACOO}, C^{\liq}_{\HCOthree}\right]
                    && \left(\unit{mol\,m^{-3}}\right)
                \end{align}
                where $\textbf{C}$ is the array of unknown true-species concentrations.
                Each equation is set to zero and the true species concentrations are obtained as the roots of the nonlinear algebraic system. Concentration is used as the unknown variable because the routine validation campaign assumes unit activity coefficients in the equilibrium equations and because concentration gives better numerical scaling than mole fraction for dilute $\COtwo$ species. Since the true mole fraction of $\COtwo$ can become small near the top of the column, scaling both the unknowns and equations is important for root-finder convergence. The calculation stores the previous converged chemical-equilibrium solution and reuses it as the next initial guess while marching through the profile. This sequential initialization reduces the number of failed local chemistry solves during BVP residual evaluation.

                The routine validation campaign uses this concentration-based equilibrium calculation. It assumes unit activity coefficients in the equilibrium equations but still produces true ionic species concentrations for $\COtwo$, MEA, H$_2$O, MEAH$^+$, MEACOO$^-$, and HCO$_3^-$. Those true species states are passed to the transport-property and ePC-SAFT fugacity calculations. Thus, the routine ePC-SAFT campaign changes the CO$_2$ fugacity closure, not the chemical-equilibrium model. A separate activity-coupled speciation path was also tested by using ePC-SAFT activity-related quantities inside the reaction-equilibrium solve. That path is technically feasible but too slow for the repeated benchmark runs targeted here, so it is reported as feasibility and timing evidence rather than as the routine validation model. Once true species concentrations are known, VLE behavior can be evaluated.


            \subsubsection{Vapor-Liquid Equilibrium}
                The vapor--liquid equilibrium calculation determines the $\COtwo$ driving force for absorption in the column. This driving force is the difference between vapor and liquid fugacities. One detailed activity-coefficient route is the eNRTL model originally developed by Zhang et al. \cite{Zhang2011}. In that model, binary electrolyte interaction parameters between molecules and electrolytes are fitted with speciation data, and thermodynamic properties such as heat capacity and excess enthalpy can be derived. This model is widely used for the $\MEA-\HtwoO-\COtwo$ system, but the parameter count and calibration burden can make repeated benchmark runs harder. SAFT-family equations of state provide another route for associating systems \cite{Gross2001,Cameretti2005}, and ePC-SAFT adds electrolyte terms needed for ionic liquid states. SAFT-family parameters for $\COtwo$ solubility in aqueous MEA have been reported by Fakouri Baygi and Pahlavanzadeh \cite{Baygi2015}. The benchmark keeps the Henry-law model as the fast baseline and adds an ePC-SAFT driving-force calculation to evaluate how an EOS-based CO$_2$ fugacity-coefficient calculation changes the solved column predictions when it is placed directly inside the absorber driving-force term. The following outlines the derivation and assumptions for relating liquid fugacity to Henry's Law. The equilibrium definition for a species between liquid and vapor phases is
                \begin{equation}
                    f_i^v = f_i^l
                    \qquad \left(\unit{Pa}\right)
                \end{equation}
                
                Evaluate both fugacities in terms of fugacity/activity coefficients:
                \begin{equation}
                    y_i \, \hat{\phi}_i^v \, P = x_i \, \gamma_i \, f_i^\circ
                    \qquad \left(\unit{Pa}\right)
                \end{equation}
                
                If $i$ is an infinitely dilute solute such that:
                \begin{equation}
                    \lim_{x_i \to 0} \left( \frac{f_i^l}{x_i} \right) = H_{i,j}
                    \qquad \left(\unit{Pa}\right)
                \end{equation}
                
                Then $f_i^l \approx x_i H_{i,j}$, and therefore $H_{i,j} = \gamma_i \, f_i^\circ$. Substituting this relation and assuming an ideal vapor phase gives
                \begin{equation}
                    y_i\, P = x_i H_{i,j}
                    \qquad \left(\unit{Pa}\right)
                \end{equation}
                
                Henry's law can also be written in concentration form:
                \begin{equation}
                    y_i \, P = C_i \, H^{c}_{i,j}
                    \qquad \left(\unit{Pa}\right)
                \end{equation}
                
                Therefore, we obtain the simplified expressions:
                \begin{align}
                    f_i^v &= y_i P
                    && \left(\unit{Pa}\right)\\
                    f_i^l &= C_i H^{c}_{i,j}
                    && \left(\unit{Pa}\right)
                \end{align}

                In an activity-coefficient model, Henry's law can determine the solute activity coefficient through the infinite-dilution relation $H_{i,j} = \gamma^{\infty}_i f^{\circ}_i$.
                
                The following equations summarize the Henry-law fugacity expressions used in the baseline model for $\COtwo$.
                \begin{align}
                    \textit{f}^{\:\vap}_{ \COtwo} &= P y_{\COtwo} && \left(\unit{Pa} \right)\\
                    \textit{f}^{\:\liq}_{ \COtwo} &= C^{\liq}_{ \COtwo, \text{true}} H_{\COtwo,\text{mix}} &&\left(\unit{Pa} \right) \label{eq:ideal}
                \end{align}
                The parameters used to determine the Henry's Law expression for $\COtwo$ in the liquid mixture are tuned to capture the main mixture interactions and are fitted with speciation data. This baseline is simpler than an eNRTL activity-coefficient model \cite{Zhang2011} or an ePC-SAFT equation-of-state model, and it is stable when the benchmark needs to run many cases. The ePC-SAFT calculation is introduced as a comparison model, not as a claim that it is faster than Henry's Law or that it replaces the chemical-equilibrium model.

                \subsubsection*{ePC-SAFT fugacity-coefficient calculation}
                The ePC-SAFT calculation is used here as a residual-Helmholtz equation of state for the fugacity coefficient of $\COtwo$, while the absorber chemistry remains the concentration-based calculation described above. For the liquid phase, the local MEA electrolyte state activates the ionic terms. For the vapor phase, the same ePC-SAFT machinery is used but the electrolyte terms vanish because the vapor composition contains only neutral species. The selected calculation uses linear relative-permittivity mixing and the classic Born contribution, with no fitted Born diameter, solvation-shell correction, or dielectric-saturation correction in the routine validation model. For each phase state $(T,P,\mathbf{x})$, the equation of state solves for the phase density and evaluates the residual Helmholtz energy as $\tilde{a}^{res}=\tilde{a}^{hc}+\tilde{a}^{disp}+\tilde{a}^{assoc}+\tilde{a}^{DH}+\tilde{a}^{Born}$. The hard-chain term accounts for excluded-volume and chain-connectivity effects, the dispersion term accounts for attractive interactions, the association term accounts for site-specific association such as hydrogen bonding, the Debye--Huckel term accounts for long-range ionic screening, and the Born term accounts for ion-solvation work. The five residual-Helmholtz contribution terms evaluated by the ePC-SAFT calculation are summarized as
                \begin{align}
                    \tilde{a}^{hc}
                    &= \bar{m}\tilde{a}^{hs}
                    -\sum_i x_i(m_i-1)\ln g_{ii}^{hs}(\sigma_{ii})
                    && \text{dimensionless}\label{eq:epcsaft-hc}\\
                    \tilde{a}^{disp}
                    &= -2\pi\rho I_1(\eta,\bar{m})\overline{m^2\epsilon\sigma^3}
                    -\pi\rho\bar{m}C_1I_2(\eta,\bar{m})\overline{m^2\epsilon^2\sigma^3}
                    && \text{dimensionless}\label{eq:epcsaft-disp}\\
                    \tilde{a}^{assoc}
                    &= \sum_i x_i\sum_{A_i}
                    \left(\ln X^{A_i}-\frac{X^{A_i}}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\right)
                    && \text{dimensionless}\label{eq:epcsaft-assoc}\\
                    \tilde{a}^{DH}
                    &=-\frac{\kappa e^{2}}{12\pi\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon_{r}k_{B}T}
                    \sum_i x_i z_i^2 \chi_i
                    && \text{dimensionless}\label{eq:epcsaft-dh}\\
                    \tilde{a}^{Born}
                    &=
                    -\frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 k_B T}
                    \sum_i x_i z_i^2
                    \sum_{\delta\in\mathcal{D}}
                    \left(1-\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{r,m_\delta}}\right)
                    D_{i,\delta}^{(m_\delta)}
                    && \text{dimensionless}\label{eq:epcsaft-born}.
                \end{align}
                Here $\bar{m}$ is the mean segment number, $\rho$ is molar density, $\eta$ is the packing fraction, $X^{A_i}$ is the fraction of nonbonded association sites, $\kappa$ and $\chi_i$ are the Debye--Huckel screening quantities, and $D_{i,\delta}^{(m_\delta)}$ collects the segment-diameter radius terms used by the classic Born contribution. The auxiliary definitions, derivative expressions, and mixing rules follow the cited SAFT-family and ePC-SAFT formulations \cite{Gross2001,Cameretti2005,Held2008,Held2014,Bulow2019,Bulow2020,Bulow2021a}. They are not repeated here because the present paper uses ePC-SAFT as a thermodynamic closure inside the absorber benchmark rather than deriving the equation of state itself. All quantities are evaluated at the local phase state $(T,P,\mathbf{x})$ after the density solve. The parameter record used for the ePC-SAFT runs is summarized in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-properties}.

                The residual Helmholtz energy is then differentiated by the ePC-SAFT implementation to obtain residual chemical potentials, compressibility factors, and fugacity coefficients.
                The ePC-SAFT calculation then uses separate vapor- and liquid-side fugacity coefficients,
                \begin{align}
                    f_{\COtwo}^{\vap} &= y_{\COtwo}\phi_{\COtwo}^{\vap}P
                    && \left(\unit{Pa}\right),\\
                    f_{\COtwo}^{\liq} &= x_{\COtwo}^{\liq}\phi_{\COtwo}^{\liq}P
                    && \left(\unit{Pa}\right).
                \end{align}
                This keeps the comparison narrow: only the CO$_2$ fugacity driving force changes, while the same column balances, reaction speciation, enhancement factor, and transport correlations are retained. The result is a direct column-level test of whether a liquid-side ionic ePC-SAFT fugacity calculation can be used quickly enough to support absorber-simulation studies.
                The corresponding $\HtwoO$ fugacity expressions are
                \begin{align}
                    \textit{f}^{\:\vap}_{ \HtwoO} &= P y_{\HtwoO} && \left(\unit{Pa} \right)\\
                    \textit{f}^{\:\liq}_{ \HtwoO} &= P_{\text{sat}, \HtwoO} x_{\HtwoO, \text{true}} && \left(\unit{Pa} \right)
                \end{align}
                and these equations are retained because $\HtwoO$ is the abundant solvent and the routine comparison is localized to the CO$_2$ driving-force closure. The H$_2$O flux therefore uses the same vapor-pressure-based expressions in both thermodynamic campaigns. The vapor--liquid fugacity difference sets the thermodynamic part of the mass-transfer rate, while the transfer coefficients, interfacial area, and enhancement correction determine how that driving force changes the local column profiles.

        \subsection{Transport}\label{subsec:transport}
            Transport and hydraulic correlations convert local temperature, composition, pressure, and flow rates into interfacial area, liquid holdup, phase velocities, mass-transfer coefficients, heat-transfer coefficients, and pressure drop. These quantities determine both the resistance to $\COtwo$ transfer and the heat exchange between phases. They are also a major source of numerical stiffness because the correlations are meaningful only inside a restricted physical domain: positive densities and viscosities, positive diffusivities, physically meaningful holdup, nonzero vapor void fraction, and bounded temperatures. Successful benchmark rows are interpreted together with boundary residuals and convergence information, not from solver success alone.

            \subsubsection{Hydraulic Variables and Correlations}
                \paragraph{Velocity}\mbox{}\\[0.5em]
                The superficial velocities of each phase are calculated from molar flow rate, column area, and molar density:
                \begin{align}
                    u_l &= \frac{F^{\liq}_{T}}{A \,\rho^{\liq}_{ \text{mol}}}
                    && \left(\unit{m\,s^{-1}}\right)\\
                    u_v &= \frac{F^{\vap}_{T}}{A \,\rho^{\vap}_{ \text{mol}}}
                    && \left(\unit{m\,s^{-1}}\right).
                \end{align}

                \paragraph{Interfacial Area}\mbox{}\\[0.5em]
                The effective interfacial area converts packed-bed geometry and liquid wetting into available transfer area per bed volume. It depends on liquid density, surface tension, superficial velocity, and packing geometry, and it enters both the molar-flux expression and the mass-transfer correlations reported by Tsai~\cite{Tsai2010}.
                \begin{align}
                    a_e 
                    &= 1.42\,a_p\left[\frac{\rho_L}{\sigma_L}\,g^{1/3}\left(\frac{u_L}{a_p}\right)^{4/3}\right]^{0.116} && \left(\unit{m^{-1}}\right)
                \end{align}

                \paragraph{Liquid Holdup}\mbox{}\\[0.5em]
                The liquid holdup is the volume of liquid held per volume of a packed bed under operating conditions. It is found in each of the mass transfer coefficient equations given by Chinen and Tsai~\cite{Chinen2018,Tsai2010}
                \begin{align}
                    h_L &= A\left[u_L \alpha\left(\frac{\mu_L}{\rho_L}\right)^{1 / 3}\right]^{B} && \text{dimensionless}\\
                    h_V &= \epsilon - h_L && \text{dimensionless}
                \end{align}
                Here $A$ and $B$ are correlation parameters, and $\alpha$ combines packing-specific constants.

                \paragraph{Mass Transfer Coefficients}\mbox{}\\[0.5em]
                The liquid and vapor mass-transfer coefficients are evaluated from correlations that depend on phase density, viscosity, diffusivity, superficial velocity, packing area, void fraction, and packing-specific constants. The coefficients and constants follow regression-based packed-column correlations reported by Billet and Schultes and by Chinen and co-workers~\cite{Billet1999,Chinen2018}.
                \begin{align}
                    k_l &= C_L
                           \left(\frac{g\rho^{\liq}}{\mu^{\liq}}\right)^{1/6}
                           \left(\frac{1}{d_h}\right)^{1/2}
                           \left(\frac{u^{\liq}}{a_p}\right)^{1/3}
                           {D^{\liq}}^{1/2}
                           && \left(\frac{\unit{m} }{\unit{s}} \right)\\
                    k_v &= \frac{C^{\vap}}{R\,T_v}
                           \left(\frac{a_p}{d_h\,h_V}\right)^{1/2}  
                           \left(\frac{u^{\vap}}{\rho^{\vap}\,a_p\,\mu^{\vap}}\right)^{3 / 4}
                           {D^{\vap}}^{2 / 3}
                           \left(\frac{\mu^{\vap}}{\rho^{\vap}}\right)^{1 / 3}
                           && \left(\frac{\unit{mol} }{\unit{Pa} \cdot{m}^2 \cdot \unit{s}} \right)
                \end{align}

                \paragraph{Heat Transfer Coefficient}\mbox{}\\[0.5em]
                The overall heat-transfer coefficient is estimated with the Chilton--Colburn analogy:
                \begin{align}
                    U_T = \left(\frac{(k^{\vap}_{\COtwo}\,P)^3\,{k_{t}^{\vap}}^{2}\,C^{\vap}_{p,T}}{\rho^{\vap}_{\text{mol}}\,{D^{\vap}_{\COtwo}}^2}\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}
                    && \left(\unit{J\,s^{-1}\,m^{-2}\,K^{-1}}\right)
                \end{align}

                \paragraph{Enhancement Factor}\mbox{}\\[0.5em]
                The enhancement factor is defined as the ratio of reactive absorption to physical absorption. It accounts for the additional liquid-side driving force introduced by the MEA reaction chemistry without solving a full film reaction-diffusion problem at every axial point. The benchmark uses an explicit pseudo-second-order approximation based on Gaspar's enhancement-factor model \cite{Gaspar2015}. The rate expression and Hatta number are
                \begin{align}
                    k_2 &= 3.1732 \times 10^{9} \exp\left( \frac{-4936.6}{T_l}\right)
                    && \left(\unit{m^{3}\,mol^{-1}\,s^{-1}}\right)\\
                    Ha &= \frac{\sqrt{k_2 C^{\liq}_{\MEA,true} D^{\liq}_{\COtwo}}}{k^{\liq}_{\COtwo}}
                    && \text{dimensionless}
                \end{align}
                where $k_2$ is the pseudo-second-order rate coefficient and $Ha$ compares the rate of reaction in the liquid film to diffusion through that film. The dimensionless resistance terms are
                \begin{align}
                    R_+ &= \frac{D^{\liq}_{\MEA} C^{\liq}_{\MEA,true}}
                    {2D^{\liq}_{\MEAH} C^{\liq}_{\MEAH,true}}
                    && \text{dimensionless}\\
                    R_- &= \frac{D^{\liq}_{\MEA} C^{\liq}_{\MEA,true}}
                    {2D^{\liq}_{\MEACOO} C^{\liq}_{\MEACOO,true}}
                    && \text{dimensionless}\\
                    \widehat{E} &= \frac{D^{\liq}_{\MEA} C^{\liq}_{\MEA,true}}
                    {2D^{\liq}_{\COtwo} C^{\liq}_{\COtwo,true}}
                    && \text{dimensionless}
                \end{align}
                which gives the explicit enhancement factor
                \begin{align}
                    E &= 1+\frac{Ha-1}{1 + Ha(R_+ + R_- + 2)/\widehat{E}}
                    && \text{dimensionless}.
                \end{align}
                The corresponding CO$_2$ driving-force correction is written as
                \begin{align}
                    \Psi &= E \frac{k^{\liq}_{ \COtwo}}{k^{\vap}_{ \COtwo}}
                    && \left(\unit{Pa\,m^{3}\,mol^{-1}}\right)\\
                    \Psi_H &= \frac{\Psi}{\Psi + H_{\COtwo, \text{mix}}}
                    && \text{dimensionless},
                \end{align}
                The routine campaign uses this expression directly in both the Henry-law and ePC-SAFT campaigns. It is not adjusted by case or by thermodynamic model, so the benchmark compares thermodynamic closures without introducing a replacement mass-transfer tuning factor.

            \subsubsection{Flux}
                \paragraph{Molar Flux}\mbox{}\\[0.5em]
                Interfacial molar flux determines how $\COtwo$ and $\HtwoO$ transfer between phases along the packed height.
                \begin{align}
                    N^{\vap}_{ \COtwo} &=  -k^{\vap}_{ \COtwo}  a_e A  \left(\textit{f}^{\:\vap}_{ \COtwo} - \textit{f}^{\:\liq}_{ \COtwo}\right) \Psi _{H} && \left(\frac{\unit{mol}}{\unit{s\cdot m}} \right) \\
                    N^{\liq}_{ \COtwo} &= -N^{\vap}_{ \COtwo} && \left(\frac{\unit{mol}}{\unit{s\cdot m}} \right) \\
                    N^{\vap}_{ \HtwoO} &= -k^{\vap}_{ \HtwoO} a_e A \left(\textit{f}^{\:\vap}_{ \HtwoO} - \textit{f}^{\:\liq}_{ \HtwoO}\right)  && \left(\frac{\unit{mol}}{\unit{s\cdot m}} \right) \\
                    N^{\liq}_{ \HtwoO} &= -N^{\vap}_{ \HtwoO} && \left(\frac{\unit{mol}}{\unit{s\cdot m}} \right)
                \end{align}
                Here flux is defined as a material flux $\left(\frac{\unit{mol}}{\unit{s\cdot m}} \right)$ per packed-column length. The specific packing area maps interfacial transfer onto the one-dimensional bed coordinate. The flux equations combine transport terms, the fugacity driving force, and, for $\COtwo$, a reaction-enhancement correction. Because absorbed $\COtwo$ reacts in the liquid phase, the CO$_2$ flux includes the enhancement correction $\Psi_H$.

                \paragraph{Enthalpy Flux}\mbox{}\\[0.5em]
                The interphase enthalpy source contains the enthalpy carried by transferred CO$_2$ and H$_2$O plus sensible heat exchange between phases. Heat transfer is modeled with the same effective interfacial area and the overall heat-transfer coefficient $U$:
                \begin{align}
                    q^{\vap} &= -U a_e A \left(T_v-T_l\right) && \left(\frac{\unit{J}}{\unit{s\cdot m}} \right) \\
                    q^{\liq} &= -U a_e A \left(T_l-T_v\right) && \left(\frac{\unit{J}}{\unit{s\cdot m}} \right) \\
                    \dot{H}^{\vap}_{flux} &=   N^{\vap}_{ \COtwo}H^{\vap}_{ \COtwo} + N^{\vap}_{ \HtwoO}H^{\vap}_{ \HtwoO} +  q^{\vap} && \left(\frac{\unit{J}}{\unit{s\cdot m}} \right) \\
                    \dot{H}^{\liq}_{flux} &= -\left(N^{\vap}_{ \COtwo}H^{\vap}_{ \COtwo} + N^{\vap}_{ \HtwoO}H^{\vap}_{ \HtwoO}\right) +  q^{\liq} && \left(\frac{\unit{J}}{\unit{s\cdot m}} \right)
                \end{align}

                The enthalpy transferred is the product of molar enthalpy and molar flux of the transferring species ($\COtwo, \HtwoO$). The liquid-side enthalpy source is written as the negative vapor-side transferred enthalpy plus heat transfer so that interphase enthalpy transfer is conservative. There is also heat transfer governed by the overall heat-transfer coefficient $U$, interfacial area $\left(a_e\right)$, cross-sectional area $\left(A\right)$, and the temperature gradient.
                
        \subsection{Governing Equations}\label{subsec:governing-equations}
            The governing equations combine the thermodynamic driving force, transport correlations, reaction enhancement, and packed-column balances. The numerical state contains the apparent CO$_2$ and H$_2$O molar flows in each phase, phase enthalpy flows, and pressure. This state vector is used by the shooting, finite-difference, and collocation BVP formulations so that method comparisons are made with the same model equations.

            \subsubsection{Material Balance}\label{subsubsec:material-balance}
                Axial material balances equate the change in apparent molar flow to the interfacial flux over the packed height. Only CO$_2$ and H$_2$O are transferred between phases in the differential state:
                \begin{align}
                    \frac{d F^{\liq}_{ \COtwo}}{d z} &= -N^{\liq}_{ \COtwo} && \left(\frac{\unit{mol}}{\unit{s\cdot m}} \right)\\
                    \frac{d F^{\liq}_{ \HtwoO}}{d z} &= -N^{\liq}_{ \HtwoO} && \left(\frac{\unit{mol}}{\unit{s\cdot m}} \right)\\
                    \frac{d F^{\vap}_{ \COtwo}}{d z} &= N^{\vap}_{ \COtwo} && \left(\frac{\unit{mol}}{\unit{s\cdot m}} \right)\\
                    \frac{d F^{\vap}_{ \HtwoO}}{d z} &= N^{\vap}_{ \HtwoO} && \left(\frac{\unit{mol}}{\unit{s\cdot m}} \right)
                \end{align}
                MEA, N$_2$, and O$_2$ are treated as non-transferring apparent components over the one-bed benchmark. Phase compositions and loading are reconstructed from the solved flow variables:
                \begin{align}
                    x_i &= \frac{F^{\liq}_{ i}}{F^{\liq}_{ T}}
                    && \text{dimensionless}\\
                    y_i &= \frac{F^{\vap}_{ i}}{F^{\vap}_{ T}}
                    && \text{dimensionless}\\
                    \alpha &= \frac{x_{\COtwo}}{x_{\MEA}}
                    && \text{dimensionless}
                \end{align}

            \subsubsection{Energy Balance}\label{subsubsec:energy-balance}
                The thermal state is written as phase enthalpy flow. Interfacial heat and material transfer enter through the enthalpy source terms:
                \begin{align}
                    \frac{d \dot{H}^{\liq}}{d z} &= \dot{H}^{\liq}_{flux} && \left(\frac{\unit{J}}{\unit{s\cdot m}} \right)\\
                    \frac{d \dot{H}^{\vap}}{d z} &= \dot{H}^{\vap}_{flux} && \left(\frac{\unit{J}}{\unit{s\cdot m}} \right)
                \end{align}
                The reported temperature profiles are obtained from the same enthalpy state. For phase $j$, with $\dot{H}^{j}=F^{j}H^{j}$,
                \begin{align}
                    \frac{dT^j}{dz} &= \frac{\dot{H}^{j}_{flux} - \frac{dF^j}{dz} H^j}{F^j \cdot \frac{dH^j}{dT^j}}
                    && \left(\unit{K\,m^{-1}}\right)
                \end{align}
                Substituting the phase flow derivatives gives the equivalent temperature-gradient form:
                \begin{align}
                    \frac{dT_l}{dz} &= \frac{\dot{H}^{\mathrm{liq}}_{flux} +  H_l(N^{\mathrm{liq}}_{\mathrm{CO}_2} + N^{\mathrm{liq}}_{\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}})}{F_l \cdot \frac{dH_l}{dT_l}}
                    && \left(\unit{K\,m^{-1}}\right)\\[1em]
                    \frac{dT_v}{dz} &= \frac{\dot{H}^{\mathrm{vap}}_{flux} -  H_v(N^{\mathrm{vap}}_{\mathrm{CO}_2} + N^{\mathrm{vap}}_{\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}})}{F_v \cdot C^{\mathrm{vap}}_{p}}
                    && \left(\unit{K\,m^{-1}}\right)
                \end{align}
                Since the vapor enthalpy is based on heat capacity, its temperature derivative reduces to $C_p^{\vap}$. The liquid derivative $\frac{dH_l}{dT_l}$ is evaluated from the liquid enthalpy correlation because the liquid enthalpy expression includes additional temperature-dependent terms. If the numerical coordinate is normalized by packed height, each right-hand side is multiplied by the total packed height.
                
            \subsubsection{Pressure State}
                Pressure is retained as a state variable so that pressure-drop extensions can use the same state layout. For the one-bed benchmark calculations reported here,
                \begin{align}
                    \frac{dP}{dz} = 0
                    && \left(\unit{Pa\,m^{-1}}\right).
                \end{align}

                The resulting state vector and residual function are
                \begin{align}
                    \mathbf{y}(z) &=
                    \begin{pmatrix}
                        F^{\liq}_{\mathrm{CO_2}}(z)\\[4pt]
                        F^{\liq}_{\mathrm{H_2O}}(z)\\[4pt]
                        F^{\vap}_{\mathrm{CO_2}}(z)\\[4pt]
                        F^{\vap}_{\mathrm{H_2O}}(z)\\[4pt]
                        \dot{H}^{\liq}(z)\\[4pt]
                        \dot{H}^{\vap}(z)\\[4pt]
                        P(z)
                    \end{pmatrix}
                    && \left(
                    4\times\unit{mol\,s^{-1}},
                    2\times\unit{J\,s^{-1}},
                    \unit{Pa}
                    \right)^{\mathsf{T}},\\[1em]
                    \frac{d\mathbf{y}}{dz} &=
                    \begin{pmatrix}
                        \frac{d F^{\liq}_{ \COtwo}}{d z} \\[4pt]
                        \frac{d F^{\liq}_{ \HtwoO}}{d z} \\[4pt]
                        \frac{d F^{\vap}_{ \COtwo}}{d z} \\[4pt]
                        \frac{d F^{\vap}_{ \HtwoO}}{d z} \\[4pt]
                        \frac{d \dot{H}^{\liq}}{d z} \\[4pt]
                        \frac{d \dot{H}^{\vap}}{d z}\\[4pt]
                        \frac{dP}{dz}
                    \end{pmatrix}
                    \equiv
                    \mathbf{f}(z, \mathbf{y})
                    && \left(
                    4\times\unit{mol\,s^{-1}\,m^{-1}},
                    2\times\unit{J\,s^{-1}\,m^{-1}},
                    \unit{Pa\,m^{-1}}
                    \right)^{\mathsf{T}}.
                \end{align}
                Convergence is evaluated from the boundary residuals and validation metrics rather than from the nonlinear solver message alone.

            \subsubsection{Boundary Conditions}
                The equations are solved as a boundary-value problem because the vapor enters at the bottom and the lean liquid enters at the top. Let $a=0$ denote the vapor inlet and $b=H$ denote the liquid inlet. The seven differential states require seven boundary conditions:
                \begin{align}
                    \boldsymbol{\alpha} &= \left[F^{\vap}_{ \COtwo, a}, F^{\vap}_{ \HtwoO, a}, \dot{H}^{\vap}_{ a}, P_a\right]
                    && \left(
                    2\times\unit{mol\,s^{-1}},
                    \unit{J\,s^{-1}},
                    \unit{Pa}
                    \right)^{\mathsf{T}}\\
                    \boldsymbol{\beta} &= \left[F^{\liq}_{ \COtwo, b}, F^{\liq}_{ \HtwoO, b}, \dot{H}^{\liq}_{ b}\right]
                    && \left(
                    2\times\unit{mol\,s^{-1}},
                    \unit{J\,s^{-1}}
                    \right)^{\mathsf{T}}
                \end{align}
                or equivalently,
                \begin{align}
                    \mathbf{r}(\mathbf{y}) &=
                    \begin{pmatrix}
                        \mathbf{y}_{\vap}(a)-\boldsymbol{\alpha}\\
                        \mathbf{y}_{\liq}(b)-\boldsymbol{\beta}
                    \end{pmatrix}
                    =
                    \mathbf{0}
                    && \left(
                    2\times\unit{mol\,s^{-1}},
                    \unit{J\,s^{-1}},
                    \unit{Pa},
                    2\times\unit{mol\,s^{-1}},
                    \unit{J\,s^{-1}}
                    \right)^{\mathsf{T}}.
                \end{align}
